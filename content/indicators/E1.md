---
takeaway: The share of business income paid to workers has been falling for decades, long before generative AI. The question this page tracks is whether that fall speeds up as AI spreads.
---

## Why this matters for gradual disempowerment

The gradual disempowerment argument (Kulveit et al., 2025) starts from a simple observation: economies serve people partly because they need people's work. Workers who are needed have bargaining power. If AI systems can do more of the work, firms need fewer workers, and that source of leverage weakens.

The labor share is the most direct public measure of how much of what businesses produce is paid out to the people who work in them. Chooi, Lee and Li (2026) call labor cost share "the most direct financial measure of human economic participation." If AI substitutes for human work at scale, a falling labor share is one place the change should show up.

This indicator sits in the **erosion** row: it asks whether the economy's dependence on human labor is falling, and by how much. It is a **lagging** indicator. Income shares move slowly, so it is more likely to confirm a change already under way than to warn of one early.

## What it shows

- **Labor share of income, nonfarm business sector.** Published quarterly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS series PRS85006174), as a percent of current-dollar output, seasonally adjusted. The chart shows the full history from 1947.
- **Profit share, for context.** Corporate profits after tax as a share of gross domestic product, from the Bureau of Economic Analysis through FRED. It uses a different numerator and denominator, so it is a rough counterpart to the labor share and does not add up to 100 with it. The labor share alone drives this indicator's reading.

The comparison is **structural**. The baseline is the series' own pattern in 2010 to 2019, after the 2008 to 2009 recession and before COVID. The question for a reading is whether the series is departing from that pattern, and how fast. Readings start in Stage 2, once the threshold rule is frozen. Until then the reading shows "Not yet reviewed."

## What it doesn't show

- **It does not measure AI.** The labor share responds to globalization, market concentration, the decline of unions, the mix of industries, the business cycle and more. Chooi, Lee and Li note that labor cost share has been declining since the 1980s for reasons like these. That is why its AI-specificity badge is **weak**: the decline began long before generative AI, there is no AI-exposed comparison group in this series, and the known confounders are not addressed.
- **It does not measure leverage directly.** Bargaining power is the thing the argument is about. The labor share is a proxy for it.
- **It does not measure welfare.** People can be materially better off while losing leverage, and the reverse can also happen. A falling labor share in a fast-growing economy can come with rising real wages. A stable share can come with stagnant ones. This site tracks influence and dependence, and a material-welfare signal belongs in the counter-evidence panel, which shows one.
- **It covers the nonfarm business sector only.** Government, farms, nonprofit institutions and private households are outside it.

## What would change our reading

**Direction assumption.** A falling labor share would be consistent with eroding human leverage in the economy. A stable or rising share points the other way, with the caveat below.

**Main rival reading.** Labor share could hold steady while work moves into sectors where workers have less leverage. Alex Imas describes a "relational sector," where "human involvement in the role is part of the value," that "may expand as labor flows to existing relational jobs (e.g., healthcare, education)." If labor flows into such jobs as AI takes over other work, labor's share of income could stay roughly flat. So a flat line is not proof that leverage is intact.

**What would raise our confidence that a change is AI-related:**

- A fall that begins or speeds up after late 2022 and departs from the 2010 to 2019 pattern by more than its normal variation.
- A faster fall in AI-exposed industries than in less-exposed ones. The site plans this comparison for Release 2, using BEA industry data with published AI-exposure weights.

**What would lower it:**

- A fall that tracks the business cycle.
- A fall that is concentrated in industries with little AI exposure.
- A fall that reverses when the economy slows.

Like every indicator on this site, this one shows conditions under which the hypothesized mechanism would operate. It does not prove that disempowerment is happening.

## Caveats

- **BLS revises recent quarters.** For example, BLS first published the labor share for the second quarter of 2026 as 52.8 percent. As of 17 November 2026, the same quarter reads 53.3 percent. The site uses the latest revised data, so recent values can move after a reading is made. A revision that changes a proposed reading is treated as new data and goes to review. A later version will show readings computed with data as first published alongside the revised data (from ALFRED vintages).
- **The percent level comes from BLS directly.** FRED carries only an index version of this series, so the site reads the percent level from the BLS public API.
- **Quarter-to-quarter movements are noisy.** Readings use a fixed window and a threshold rule, set in Stage 2, so a single quarter cannot by itself change a reading.
- **The retrospective is not yet computed.** Readings for each quarter from the fourth quarter of 2022 onward will be computed once, with the frozen rule, and published as a dated snapshot in Stage 3.
