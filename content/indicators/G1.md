## Why this matters for gradual disempowerment

A state that funds itself by taxing its citizens' work has a reason to keep them productive and to listen to them. A state that funds itself from other sources has less reason to. Political scientists call this the rentier-state pattern. It is a common explanation for why governments that live on oil revenue tend to be less accountable to their citizens.

Kulveit et al. (2025) apply the same logic to AI. If AI shifts income from wages toward capital, and tax revenue follows, the state could come to depend less on its citizens. Chooi, Lee and Li (2026) propose a related metric: citizen tax revenue relative to AI-derived corporate profits.

This indicator is the cleanest public test of that state-dependence argument. It sits in the **erosion** row, as the core mechanism for the government domain. It is a **lagging** indicator: tax structures change slowly.

**What the data says now.** The OECD context points away from disempowerment. Across OECD countries, the labor-based share of tax revenue has not fallen. It was 48.53 percent in 2010, peaked at 50.54 percent in 2020, and was 49.20 percent in 2023, the latest year with an OECD average.

The US quarterly series drives this indicator's reading. It has fallen since 2022: it averaged 66.85 percent in 2022 and was 63.21 percent in the second quarter of 2026 (data as of 17 November 2026). That is still inside the range it moved through in 2010 to 2019 (62.30 to 66.88 percent).

Taken together, the evidence does not currently point toward disempowerment. The US decline is worth watching. The reading itself is not yet reviewed.

## What it shows

- **United States, quarterly (drives the reading).** The labor-based share of tax revenue, from the Bureau of Economic Analysis national accounts through FRED:
  - The numerator is personal current taxes plus contributions for government social insurance.
  - The denominator is government current tax receipts plus those contributions.
  - It covers all levels of government (federal, state and local), seasonally adjusted.
- **United States and OECD average, annual (context).** Taxes on individual incomes plus social security contributions, as a share of total tax revenue, from OECD Revenue Statistics. The OECD data lag about two years.

The comparison is **structural**. The baseline is the series' own pattern in 2010 to 2019. Readings start in Stage 2, once the threshold rule is frozen. Until then the reading shows "Not yet reviewed."

## What it doesn't show

- **It does not measure AI.** Tax law, the business cycle, asset prices and an aging population all move this share. The AI-specificity badge is **weak**: there is no AI-exposed comparison group, and no change has yet been shown to follow the spread of generative AI.
- **It is labor-based only approximately.** Personal income taxes also fall on interest, dividends and capital gains. A stock-market boom can raise "labor-based" revenue with no change in how much the state depends on workers. Social contributions are tied more closely to wages.
- **It does not show whether governments respond to citizens.** It measures one source of the state's dependence on people. Elections, courts, protest and a free press are other sources, and none of them is measured here.

## What would change our reading

**Direction assumption.** A falling labor-based share would be consistent with the state depending less on taxing workers. A rising or stable share points away from disempowerment.

**Main rival reading.** A state can keep citizens on side through transfers even when it no longer depends on their taxes: the rentier pattern of co-optation. A steady labor-based tax share therefore does not guarantee that citizens keep their leverage. The share could also fall while citizens keep leverage through other channels. This is the cleanest test of state dependence, not a guarantee.

**What would move the reading toward disempowerment:**

- A sustained fall in the US share beyond its 2010 to 2019 range.
- A fall that occurs while corporate and capital-income tax receipts grow faster than wage-based receipts.
- The same fall showing up in the OECD data once those data arrive.

**What would strengthen the current reading:** the US share holding inside or above its 2010 to 2019 range, and the OECD share continuing not to fall.

A later version plans a US-states comparison: states that depend heavily on severance taxes from oil, gas and coal against states that do not. That would test the rentier logic on US data.

Like every indicator on this site, this one shows conditions under which the hypothesized mechanism would operate. It does not prove that disempowerment is happening, or that it is not.

## Caveats

- **Two sources, one reading.** The US quarterly series and the OECD annual series use different definitions and do not match exactly. The US series drives the reading. When the two disagree, the owner decides and the rationale says so. Nothing is averaged.
- **Revisions.** BEA revises the national accounts, and OECD revises Revenue Statistics. The figures quoted on this page are as of 17 November 2026, and the chart always shows the latest data.
- **The OECD average is the published OECD aggregate.** The site does not recompute it, and its country coverage follows OECD's own definitions.
- **The retrospective is not yet computed.** Readings for each quarter from the fourth quarter of 2022 onward will be computed once, with the frozen rule, and published as a dated snapshot in Stage 3.
