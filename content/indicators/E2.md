takeaway: A growing share of US firms say they use AI. This measures exposure, how far AI is entering firms, and does not by itself show that anyone has lost leverage.

## Why this matters for gradual disempowerment

Before AI can reduce the economy's dependence on human work, firms have to adopt it. This indicator tracks that first step: the share of US businesses that report using AI. It sits in the **exposure** row of the grid, which asks whether AI is entering a system and how far it has gone.

Exposure is a precondition for the mechanism Kulveit et al. (2025) describe. It is not evidence of the mechanism itself. Rising adoption is equally consistent with AI making workers more productive and more valuable, and with AI replacing them. This indicator cannot tell those apart. It is a **leading** indicator: adoption should move before any effect on labor's share of income or on tax revenue does.

## What it shows

- **The share of US firms answering "Yes" when asked whether they used AI in the last two weeks.** From the Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS), national, all sectors.
- **Census standard errors**, shown as bands on the chart. Each estimate is dated by the end of its two-week collection period.

**Firm-weighted.** Every firm counts once, whatever its size, so a five-person shop and a large employer weigh the same. An employment-weighted measure, which counts firms by how many people they employ, answers a different question (what share of workers are at firms that use AI) and can give a different number. Adoption can differ by firm size, so the two need not move together. The site labels this series "firm-weighted" wherever it appears.

**The series break on 17 November 2025.** Census changed the question after cognitive testing:

- **Through early October 2025**, firms were asked whether they used AI "in producing goods or services."
- **From the collection that began on 17 November 2025**, they are asked whether they used it "in any of its business functions," with a new list of examples.

The broader question counts more uses as AI use, so the two segments are not comparable. The chart shows them as separate segments and does not join them. There are no estimates for the weeks between the last old-wording period and the first new-wording period.

**The renamed technology.** Census's current question text refers to "Super Intelligence or SI (formerly referred to as Artificial Intelligence or AI)." This is a change of name. The question asks about the same technology, so the site does not treat the relabel as a series break. The only break the site marks is the change of wording on 17 November 2025.

**"Do not know."** A sizeable share of firms answer "Do not know." Across the periods since the November 2025 change, that share ranged from 9.1 to 13.9 percent (as published on 17 November 2026). The site shows the "Yes" share. A shift between "Do not know" and "Yes" can move the series without any change in actual use.

The comparison is **AI-native**. The baseline is the series' own recent path: its level, how fast it is changing, and whether that pace is speeding up, over rolling 3-, 6- and 12-month windows. Readings use the exposure scale (Low, Rising, Pervasive) and start in Stage 2. Until then the reading shows "Not yet reviewed."

## What it doesn't show

- **It does not show disempowerment.** The AI-specificity badge is **strong** because this series measures AI use directly. It does not mean the series shows lost leverage. Strong here means only that a change in the series is a change in AI use.
- **It does not show how AI is used.** A firm that uses AI to help its staff (augmentation) and a firm that uses it to replace staff (displacement) both answer "Yes." Exposure that augments workers may strengthen their position. Exposure that displaces them may weaken it. The site's next indicator, E3, is the only free public data on that split, and it covers one vendor's product.
- **It does not show how much AI a firm uses.** A firm that tried one tool once and a firm that runs core operations on AI count the same.
- **It does not measure welfare.** Wider AI use may raise output and incomes while shifting who has influence, or the reverse. This series speaks to neither.

## What would change our reading

**Direction assumption.** Rising adoption would be consistent with growing AI exposure in firms. By itself it does not show lost human leverage.

**Main rival reading.** Adoption may be shallow or experimental, and may augment workers rather than displace them. If adoption keeps rising while the erosion indicators (E1 labor share, G1 labor-based tax share) hold steady, the rival reading gains weight. Exposure that rises alongside erosion signals would be consistent with the hypothesized mechanism. It would still not prove it.

Like every indicator on this site, this one shows conditions under which the hypothesized mechanism would operate. It does not prove that disempowerment is happening.

## Caveats

- **Self-reported.** Answers come from one respondent per firm, who may not know every use of AI in the business. Survey response rates and the "Do not know" share both affect the estimate.
- **Two-week reference window.** The question asks about the last two weeks, so occasional use can be missed in one period and counted in the next.
- **The 17 November 2025 break.** A reading that spans the break treats the two segments separately. Part or all of any jump at the break comes from the new wording.
- **US only, and firms only.** The series says nothing about government or household use of AI.
