## Why this matters for gradual disempowerment

Culture, in the gradual disempowerment argument, stays tied to people because people make it and pass it on (Kulveit et al., 2025). Public knowledge commons are one place this happens in the open. On Stack Overflow, people ask questions and other people answer them. On Wikipedia, people write and read a shared reference.

If people turn to AI systems in place of these communities, two things follow:

- **Less human-made knowledge.** Fewer new questions, answers and articles are produced by people.
- **Thinner communities.** The communities that maintain and correct that knowledge shrink.

AI systems also learn from these commons, so a smaller commons could leave AI-generated material with a larger role in what people learn.

This indicator sits in the **erosion** row for the cultural domain. It is a **leading** indicator: activity on these sites responds within months to changes in where people go for answers.

## What it shows

**Stack Overflow against comparison sites (drives the reading).** New questions per month on Stack Overflow, compared with new questions per month on four non-programming Stack Exchange sites:

- **Home Improvement:** questions about home repair, renovation and maintenance, from plumbing and wiring to carpentry.
- **Travel:** questions about planning trips and getting around, such as visas, routes, lodging and local customs.
- **Role-playing Games:** questions about tabletop role-playing games, including rules, game design and running a group.
- **Science Fiction & Fantasy:** questions about science fiction and fantasy books, films and shows, including plot, canon and interpretation.

**The comparison sites were chosen in advance.** The site owner approved them on 17 November 2026, under criteria fixed before any post-2019 counts were requested or viewed:

- **Launched before 2015**, to give a long history before generative AI.
- **At least 400 new questions a month in 2019**, so monthly counts are not too noisy.
- **Subject matter outside programming, IT, data and math**, whose users are directly exposed to coding assistants.
- **Mostly experiential, physical-world, creative or community-specific subject matter**, where a text chatbot is a weaker substitute.
- **A normal, active main site.**

The full selection record, including the sites considered and rejected, is in the project repository.

**The contrast.** Each month, the chart shows Stack Overflow's year-over-year percent change in new questions minus the average year-over-year percent change across the four comparison sites. This is a difference-in-differences comparison. Year-over-year changes remove the seasonal pattern in these sites. Subtracting the comparison sites removes changes that hit all Stack Exchange sites alike, such as site-wide policy changes or a general shift away from Q&A sites.

- **Each comparison site's series starts at its first full month after its public beta.**
- **The contrast starts in July 2012**, the first month in which all four comparison sites have a full year of history, so the average always covers the same four sites.

**Stack Overflow's decline began before ChatGPT.** The number of surviving questions per year peaked in the mid-2010s and fell through the late 2010s. After a rise in 2020, the fall steepened in 2021 and 2022, before ChatGPT's release at the end of 2022. The chart shows this full history. The comparison sites exist partly to separate any AI-related change from this earlier decline.

**Wikipedia (shown alongside).** Two context series:

- **English Wikipedia human pageviews per month**, from July 2015. Wikimedia's own classification of human traffic, which excludes known crawlers and detected automated traffic.
- **English Wikipedia active editors per month.** Registered, non-bot editors with five or more edits in the month.

These are shown for context and do not drive the reading.

The comparison is **structural, with a comparison group**. The baseline is the contrast's own pattern in 2010 to 2019. Because the contrast starts in July 2012, its baseline covers July 2012 to December 2019. Readings start in Stage 2, once the threshold rule is frozen. Until then the reading shows "Not yet reviewed."

## What it doesn't show

- **It does not measure human capacity or reliance on AI.** It counts activity: questions asked, pages viewed, editors editing. People may know as much as before and simply ask in a different place. The site names this gap on the methodology page.
- **It does not give the full AI effect.** AI affects the comparison sites too. People can ask a chatbot about wiring a light switch or a rule in a role-playing game. The contrast therefore measures Stack Overflow's change relative to sites that are less exposed. If AI has also reduced activity on the comparison sites, the contrast understates the effect on Stack Overflow.
- **It does not separate AI from other changes specific to Stack Overflow.** Moderation policy, community norms, the maturing of the site (many common questions are already answered) and changes in the programming tools people use can all move Stack Overflow on its own. The AI-specificity badge is **moderate**:
  - The comparison group and the timing of the steeper fall are consistent with an AI role.
  - The decline that began before ChatGPT and the site-specific causes are only partly addressed.

## What would change our reading

**Direction assumption.** Stack Overflow falling faster than the comparison sites would be consistent with people turning from human knowledge communities to AI.

**Main rival reading, for Wikipedia.** Falling human pageviews may reflect AI intermediation rather than human disengagement. People may still use Wikipedia's content, reading it through chatbots and search summaries that draw on it, without visiting the site. Fewer pageviews would then mean fewer visits, with the same reliance on human-written knowledge.

**Main rival reading, for Stack Overflow.** The site's long decline, which began years before ChatGPT, may simply be continuing. That is why the reading rests on the contrast with the comparison sites.

**What would raise our confidence that a change is AI-related:**

- The gap between Stack Overflow and the comparison sites widening after late 2022, beyond its 2012 to 2019 variation.
- The same pattern holding when each comparison site is left out in turn, a robustness check planned for Stage 2.

**What would lower it:**

- A gap that depends on one comparison site.
- A gap that opened before late 2022.
- A gap that tracks a Stack Overflow policy change.

Like every indicator on this site, this one shows conditions under which the hypothesized mechanism would operate. It does not prove that disempowerment is happening.

## Caveats

- **Wikipedia's bot-reclassification break.** In 2025 Wikimedia improved its detection of automated traffic that had been counted as human. It then reclassified its data for March to August 2025. On the new logic, human pageviews were roughly 8 percent lower than in the same months of 2024 ([Wikimedia, 17 October 2025](https://diff.wikimedia.org/2025/10/17/new-user-trends-on-wikipedia/)). Months before March 2025 keep the old classification, so some of the drop across that date reflects better bot detection. The chart marks the break at March 2025.
- **Deleted questions are not counted.** Question counts come from the Stack Exchange API as of the fetch date, so questions deleted since they were asked are missing. Older months have had more time for deletions, which may lower their counts slightly.
- **Small sites are noisier.** Each comparison site gets far fewer questions a month than Stack Overflow, so their year-over-year changes jump around more from month to month. Averaging four sites reduces this. Each site counts equally in the average, whatever its size.
- **The retrospective is not yet computed.** Readings for each quarter from the fourth quarter of 2022 onward will be computed once, with the frozen rule, and published as a dated snapshot in Stage 3.
